Affirmative Action Is Not Enough: Tackling the Invisible Roots of Gender Inequality in Japanese Academia

Affirmative action has become a widely used measure to address the underrepresentation of women in STEM and academia. From quotas in hiring to gender-balanced panels and fellowships, institutions have increasingly turned to such policies to accelerate gender parity. While these interventions have produced visible improvements in representation, they do not address a more fundamental issue: the structural and cultural barriers that prevent women from fully participating in academic life. Among these, one of the most persistent and damaging is the fact that women are often unable to raise their voices safely and meaningfully within institutional spaces.

Affirmative action serves as an emergency measure. It aims to correct historical imbalances and open doors that have long been closed. In this sense, it is both necessary and beneficial. Representation matters. It signals to younger generations that they belong, and it provides role models who can inspire others to follow. However, numbers alone are a poor proxy for inclusion. When the structural context remains hostile or indifferent to women’s voices, numerical representation risks becoming tokenistic.

In Japanese academia, this structural context is particularly entrenched. Hierarchical faculty governance structures such as the "kyoujukai" (professorial councils), often composed predominantly of senior male professors, concentrate decision-making power in small, homogeneous groups. Evaluation criteria for promotion and hiring tend to reflect male-centered career patterns, such as uninterrupted publication records and long working hours, disadvantaging those who take career breaks or work flexibly. These standards are presented as neutral but operate within deeply gendered expectations.

Moreover, specific disciplines such as natural sciences and economics remain overwhelmingly male-dominated. The lack of women is often justified through claims about "aptitude" or "interest," masking the cumulative effects of socialization, bias, and exclusionary practices. In these fields, networking is heavily male-centric. Informal spaces such as post-conference dinners or research retreats frequently exhibit gendered dynamics—male researchers talking primarily to other men, forming bonds that translate into co-authorships, invitations, and career opportunities. Women often report feeling like outsiders, not for lack of merit, but because the cultural script does not include them.

In many academic environments, women face both explicit and implicit barriers to speaking up. Formal hierarchies, male-dominated leadership, and gendered expectations combine to create a culture where female academics often feel that raising concerns, asserting ideas, or challenging norms will lead to reputational damage. They risk being labeled as overly sensitive, confrontational, or not collegial. This results in a chilling effect: women self-censor not because they lack ideas or opinions, but because the institutional cost of expression is too high. Over time, this leads to a dangerous feedback loop—silence breeds invisibility, which in turn reinforces exclusion.

Thus, any serious attempt to promote gender equity must address this deeper structural issue: the inability of many women to speak and be heard. What is needed is not just affirmative action, but structural reform. This includes establishing safe and confidential channels for reporting discrimination, mandating unconscious bias training for decision-makers, building mentorship networks that empower junior women scholars, and incentivizing male allies to actively support gender equity. These measures are not cosmetic; they are foundational.

Tackling the silencing of women is not a peripheral issue—it is central to the question of justice in academia. Without a shift in institutional culture that makes it genuinely safe and valuable for women to speak, affirmative action can only go so far. We may see more women in conferences, classrooms, and committees, but we will not hear more of their ideas, criticisms, and innovations unless we dismantle the structures that punish their voices.

In conclusion, affirmative action is a bridge, not a destination. It helps us cross a dangerous gap, but it cannot replace the work of transforming the terrain itself. We must go beyond numbers. We must ask why those numbers were low in the first place. And we must create academic spaces where women are not just present, but powerful—where they speak, are heard, and lead.
