In Praise of Handouts: Just Give People the Money

Japanese version

HIROTAKA, Fukui

We Almost Had Cash—And Then We Didn't

For a brief, shining moment in April 2024, it looked like Japan might do something bold.
The government floated the idea of a universal cash handout—a rare, glorious moment when fiscal policy and common sense seemed to be on speaking terms.

And then, poof. It vanished.
Like a limited-edition bento on sale at lunchtime.

Why? Because people didn’t like it.
A Yomiuri poll found that 76% of respondents thought handing out cash wasn’t effective.
Seventy-six percent. Against free money.

My first reaction? Are you kidding me?
Eggs cost more than your streaming subscription. We’re all budgeting like monks. And you don’t want cash?

But then... okay. I took a breath.
Maybe there’s more to this. Maybe, just maybe, people were being reasonable.

Okay, Maybe the Public Had a Point

But here’s the thing. After the initial outrage—after the yelling-into-a-pillow phase—I started to think.

Maybe people weren’t being irrational. Maybe they were being painfully, frustratingly practical.

Prices have gone up. Eggs, bentos, the daily basics—everything. And yet, people haven’t panicked. They’re still buying.
According to The Economist, even long-time bento vendors have raised prices multiple times, and customers just shrug and say, “That’s how it is.”

So maybe the public wasn’t rejecting help.
Maybe they were rejecting the wrong kind of help.

Why give out ¥50,000 in cash when what people really want is for their grocery bill to stop punching them in the wallet every week?
In that context, a tax cut—especially on consumption—feels more responsible. More grounded. Less like a political stunt and more like structural relief.

It’s not irrational.
It’s worn-out, weary common sense.

And I respect that.

But…

I Know It’s Rational, But I Still Wanted the Money

I get it.

People didn’t reject cash because they hate money. They just want their everyday costs—eggs, rent, ramen—to stop climbing.
They don’t want a flashy stimulus that feels like the government saying, “Go ahead, treat yourself—on us!”
They want relief that’s practical. Grounded. Boring, even.

Honestly? That makes sense.

But here’s the thing:

I don’t want “sense.”
I want a new rice cooker.
I want a keyboard that lights up like a disco.
I want that one absurdly expensive ice cream in the gold tub that makes me feel like I own a yacht—even though I don’t even own a balcony.

I’m not trying to optimize social welfare.
I just want ¥30,000 to recklessly spend on things I don’t technically need but emotionally require.

So yes, the public and the economists might be on the same page.
But my wishlist didn’t get the memo.

And neither did my bank account.

Final Thoughts: I Don’t Care About Multiplier Effects. I Just Want Stuff.

To be fair, there’s a rational story behind this national aversion to handouts.

Japan’s consumers aren’t naïve. They’ve noticed that prices are rising—on eggs, on bentos, on daily essentials—and they’re still buying.
According to The Economist, even when bento prices go up, customers don’t complain. They understand. They adapt.
So maybe it’s not about opposing help. Maybe it’s about wanting the right kind of help.

Rather than a sudden cash injection that might feel frivolous --- “Oh, I got ¥50,000, maybe I’ll buy that air fryer I don’t need” --- people want relief on what they already have to spend every single day.
In that light, a consumption tax cut, especially on essentials, feels more grounded. More grown-up. More responsible.

It's not necessarily emotional logic. It's economic logic --- the kind that should shape public policy more than any public opinion ever will. I know it because I'm Ph.D. student in Economics --- especially --- Macroeconomics.

I want cash. In my hand. Now. Because I have things I want to buy.
Dumb things. Beautiful things. Things that make absolutely no macroeconomic sense.

A new pair of sneakers I don’t really need,

A ridiculous amount of convenience store pudding,

A keyboard that lights up in seven colors even though I only type emails,

A backpack that makes me feel like I’m going somewhere, even when I’m not,

And the big one: a ticket to see my favorite idol group in concert.
It cost me ¥20,000. Afterward, I remember thinking, “Well, guess I’m eating cup noodles for the rest of the month.”
Little did I know, it would be their last tour in Japan.
And I’m glad I went.
I think I’ll remember that night for the rest of my life.

So yes, I'm Ph.d student in Economics. I read papers, nod at the models. But deep in my heart, the logic remains simple:

I want money. Because I want stuff. And that should be enough.
