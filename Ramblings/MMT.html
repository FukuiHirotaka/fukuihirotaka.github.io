MMT: Where Illusions Are Sold and Dreams Are Swallowed Whole

Japanese version

HIROTAKA, Fukui

There has not been a single theoretical or empirical paper grounded explicitly in Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) that has been published in a top 5 economics journal—QJE, AER, JPE, Econometrica, or RESTUD. If MMT advocates truly believe that their framework is a valid and persuasive economic theory, then the burden is on them to publish it in such peer-reviewed outlets.

Instead, they have consistently failed to make the necessary academic efforts to formalize or quantify their ideas in a way that could be incorporated into real-world institutions. In short, they dream aloud without following the scientific procedures that define legitimate scholarship.

Too often, their claims are unfalsifiable—able to explain anything and everything—which disqualifies them from being considered scientific. That is not an intellectually honest stance. It is a basic responsibility of any school of thought that claims academic credibility.

One should ask: Are there phenomena that occur under the assumptions of MMT but not under other mainstream theories? Conversely, are there phenomena that mainstream models can explain but MMT cannot? And above all, do such phenomena actually occur in the real world? These are the kinds of tests any serious theory must face—and it is MMT economists themselves who must take the lead in conducting these tests.

What makes the mainstream mainstream is precisely its ability to survive such scrutiny over time. If MMT is to stand as a serious rival to the mainstream, it must first withstand a decade or more of rigorous empirical and theoretical testing without collapsing under the weight of its own contradictions. Whether such testing will ultimately validate or refute the theory is unknown, but enduring that process is the bare minimum required to be taken seriously.

Unfortunately, in all the MMT scholarship I’ve encountered, I have seen little sign of this intellectual discipline. On the contrary, many proponents seem more eager to force their untested ideas into policy frameworks than to subject them to the hard work of validation. They prefer to talk among themselves, endlessly recycling ideas assembled decades ago by others. They ignore facts and theories that don’t fit their approach, and dismiss those who disagree with them as incompetent.

Rather than engaging with their academic peers, they appeal directly to the public by painting a vision of a bright future with no costs. This alluring narrative bypasses rigorous scrutiny. As Drumetz and Pfister (2021) argue, MMT is not a falsifiable scientific theory, but rather a political and moral statement made by those who believe in the righteousness—and feasibility—of unlimited government spending to achieve progressive ends. It is, in essence, a political manifesto. In that sense, as Krugman has put it, they are not scientists but policy promoters—peddlers of economic policy.

Let them get a paper through peer review in a top journal. Until then, their arguments are not worth engaging with. If you want your ideas to be taken seriously, bring forth a theory and evidence that can withstand rigorous academic scrutiny. Otherwise, don’t expect to be heard.

To the public: Are you really willing to entrust your country to such sloppy reasoning? That’s like putting your life in the hands of a doctor who dismisses every proven treatment. If you're okay with that, fine — but don't expect to stay healthy.
